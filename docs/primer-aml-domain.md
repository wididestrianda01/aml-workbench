# Primer: the AML domain

This is the companion to the [technical report](report.md) and to
[primer-ml.md](primer-ml.md), which covers the machine-learning side. This
primer covers the domain: what money laundering is, how the law requires banks
to detect it, what the detection patterns look like, and how an alert queue
turns into operational metrics. It assumes no prior AML knowledge and explains
every term the first time it appears. All project-specific numbers are taken
from the artifacts in `data/reports/`, generated by the pipeline commands.

Written 2026-09-04. Regulatory facts are dated; the EU package applies from
2027 and details may be amended before then.

## 1. What money laundering is

Money laundering is the process of making the proceeds of crime appear to come
from a legitimate source. Criminal proceeds in cash or untraceable digital form
cannot be spent, invested, or declared without attracting attention, so the
money has to be passed through financial activity that gives it a
clean-looking origin. The classic description splits this into three stages:

1. **Placement.** The criminal introduces illicit funds into the financial
   system: cash deposits, purchases of negotiable instruments, transfers
   through informal channels.
2. **Layering.** The funds move through a series of transactions whose purpose
   is to obscure the audit trail: transfers between accounts, currency
   exchange, rapid in-and-out payments, transactions in several
   jurisdictions.
3. **Integration.** The money re-enters the economy in a form that looks
   legitimate: loans backed by deposited proceeds, sales of assets at inflated
   prices, invoices for services never rendered.

The three stages are a teaching model rather than a strict sequence. A single
scheme can combine them, and in practice they overlap. What survives from the
model, and what matters for detection, is this: laundering produces
**unusual structural patterns in transaction data**, because the transaction
graph is shaped by the goal of obscuring origin rather than by ordinary
commercial motive. Detection ("transaction monitoring") is the search for
those patterns.

**In the project.** The two datasets encode laundering in different ways.
Elliptic (Track A) labels individual Bitcoin transactions as illicit or licit
at each of 49 two-week time steps. HI-Small (Track B) identifies entire
accounts as laundering or not, and each laundering account participates in one
of several scripted typologies (Section 4). The choice of label granularity
illuminates a real distinction: a blockchain offers clean per-transaction
labels because each address history is public, while a bank flags *customers*,
and the transaction evidence only supports or contradicts the flag.

## 2. The regulatory environment

### 2.1 Why banks must do this

Banks do not monitor transactions out of caution. They are legally required
to, and they are regulated, inspected, and fined on how well they do it. The
obligations below are why the reference implementation in this repo frames its
outputs as scored, ranked, and costed alert queues: that framing is what a
supervisor can audit.

### 2.2 The EU package

The European Union consolidated its anti-money-laundering regime in 2024 into
two instruments plus one authority:

- **Regulation (EU) 2024/1624 (AMLR)** — a directly applicable regulation (not
  a directive, so no national transposition). It obliges credit and financial
  institutions to apply risk-based customer due diligence and transaction
  monitoring, and applies from mid-2027.
- **Directive (EU) 2024/1640 (AMLD6)** — harmonizes how member states
  implement the regime and covers sectors where the regulation's
  direct-application mechanism does not reach.
- **AMLA**, the new EU Anti-Money Laundering Authority, becomes operational
  around 2025-2026 and takes over direct supervision of selected high-risk
  cross-border entities from 2028.

The central phrase for anyone building detection software is **risk-based**. The
regulations do not prescribe thresholds or algorithms; they require that the
institution understand its own risk exposure and monitor in proportion to it,
and that it can demonstrate the system works. That demonstration requirement
is what EBA Guidelines EBA/GL/2021/02 spell out: institutions must evidence
the effectiveness of their transaction monitoring, which forces measurable
detection and false-positive rates rather than a testimonial that "we monitor
all transactions".

### 2.3 Sweden

Because the surrounding portfolio context is Swedish, note the national
transposition: Finansinspektionen's regulations FFFS 2017:11 (with later
amendments) carry equivalent risk-based obligations, including documentation
and effectiveness requirements, for Swedish institutions.

### 2.4 The model-risk angle

When the monitor itself is a statistical model, a second regulatory layer
applies: the model must be governed like any other risk model. The US
reference is the Federal Reserve/OCC guidance **SR 11-7** on model risk
management, which requires independent validation, documented development, and
ongoing performance monitoring. The EU AI Act adds requirements for AI systems
in regulated financial services. This project's operational machinery
(predeclared metrics, walk-forward validation, drift monitoring, rollback
runbook, run manifests tied to code commits and MLflow runs) is a small-scale
implementation of those expectations. See `docs/rollback-runbook.md`.

### 2.5 KYC, CDD, and reporting

Three closely related obligations surround transaction monitoring:

- **KYC** ("know your customer") is the popular term for identifying and
  verifying a customer at onboarding.
- **CDD** (customer due diligence) is the legal obligation behind it:
  identifying and verifying the customer and, where relevant, the beneficial
  owner, understanding the purpose of the relationship, and conducting
  ongoing monitoring. **EDD** (enhanced due diligence) applies to
  higher-risk customers, for example politically exposed persons.
- **SAR** (suspicious activity report; STR, suspicious transaction report, in
  many jurisdictions) is the confidential report a bank files with the
  financial intelligence unit when it knows or suspects that funds are the
  proceeds of crime. Filing is mandatory and tipping off the customer is a
  criminal offence.

Transaction monitoring feeds the SAR decision. The economics of the whole
regime, discussed next, flow through the fact that every SAR costs an analyst
time.

## 3. The operational economics of monitoring

### 3.1 The alert queue

A monitoring system produces **alerts**: (customer, day, reason) records that
something looked unusual. Every alert must, by procedure, be judged by a human
analyst, who closes it, escalates it, or feeds a SAR. Alert volume is the
dominant operational cost in real deployments; industry discussions routinely
describe very large proportions of investigations ending with no SAR. This
ratio is exactly the false-positive problem, and it is why precision, the
share of alerts that are real, is the metric that matters operationally.

### 3.2 Precision at k and cost per true positive

The workbench reports **precision@k**: among the top k alerts of a ranked
queue, the share that are true positives.

$$\text{precision@}k = \frac{\lvert \{\text{true positives among top } k\}\rvert}{k}$$

Precision@k answers the question a compliance team actually faces: if we
investigate the top k alerts, how many hits do we get and what does each cost?
The operational KPIs in the triage artifact (`data/reports/triage.md`) put
concrete numbers on this for HI-Small, at an operating point of the top 500
alerts investigated at 50 USD each:

- alerts investigated: 500; true positives: 3.
- $\text{precision@500} = 3/500 = 0.0060$.
- cost per true positive: $500 \times 50\ \text{USD} / 3 \approx 8{,}333\ \text{USD}$.
- detection rate: $3/60 = 0.05$, that is 5% of the 60 laundering accounts in
  the evaluation window were reached.

Reading these together is the learning point. A queue that looks strong to a
precision-at-k metric can still be operationally brutal: here, each detected
laundering account costs four orders of magnitude more than an alert. And a
high recall that comes bundled with low precision is expensive recall. The
cost-per-true-positive curve (`docs/figures/precision_at_k.png`) makes the
tradeoff explicit instead of hiding it behind one number.

### 3.3 Why the numbers here are not "real"

HI-Small contains exactly 60 laundering accounts in the evaluation window, by
construction. Real deployment has unknown prevalence, label noise, analyst
costs that vary, and an adversary. The artifact is a teaching object, not a
calibration. Limitation 6 in the report says this plainly: the operating
point is an assumption.

## 4. Typologies: the shapes laundering takes

A **typology** is a recurring transaction pattern with a laundering purpose.
Both datasets here are built around typologies: Elliptic's illicit labels come
from transactions associated with documented enforcement cases, and HI-Small
generates its laundering accounts by simulating specific pattern programs.
Recognizing the shapes is the domain skill that makes the feature engineering
in `src/aml_workbench/graph.py` and the rules in `src/aml_workbench/rules.py`
legible.

### 4.1 Structuring (smurfing)

Large transfers are more visible than small ones, so a launderer splits one
intended movement of funds below a reporting or review threshold, across many
transactions or many accounts. The signature in data: many deposits of
similar-size amounts, just below a threshold, often with related counterpart
sets. In HI-Small the structuring scenario produced 32 alerts over 15 distinct
entities, with laundering-entity precision 0.1333 (`data/reports/alert_stats.md`).

### 4.2 Fan-in and fan-out

**Fan-out**: one account sends funds outward to many recipients in a short
window (layering: spreading proceeds). **Fan-in**: many accounts send funds
inward to one collecting account (consolidation after placement). Chains of
fan-in and fan-out together produce the layered audit trail. In the alert
statistics, the fan-in scenario (1,097 alerts, precision 0.0922) is far more
precise than fan-out on raw alert counts (14,289 alerts, 0.0251), a real
movement: consolidation is more constrained than spreading.

### 4.3 Cycles and circular flow

Funds moving in loops ("circular flow") exist in ordinary commerce
(trade-finance settlements can close a loop legitimately) but at high volume
and with matching amounts and round trips they suggest layering. The
circular-flow scenario in HI-Small fires on 211,684 rule alerts, which is more
than any other scenario, at precision 0.0164: a deliberately loose rule,
fused downstream with the account model rather than relied on alone.

### 4.4 Velocity and rapid churn

**Velocity** flags accounts whose transaction rate is anomalous for the
account's own baseline. **Rapid churn** flags money that arrives and leaves
almost immediately, so the account acts as a conduit rather than a store. Both
are time-based behaviors rather than amount-based ones; the rules engine
implements this distinction by parameterizing scenario thresholds in separate
config entries (`src/aml_workbench/config.py`).

### 4.5 Dense communities

Groups of accounts that interact almost exclusively with each other,
transaction-pattern clusters, can indicate launderer-controlled networks. The
dense-community scenario in this repo fires rarely (106 alerts) but precise
(0.1200): a strong structural sign, rare enough that each firing is worth a
look.

### 4.6 What the scenario precisions teach

Sorting the eight rule scenarios by laundering-entity precision —
structuring 0.1333, dense-community 0.1200, fan-in 0.0922, velocity 0.0709,
fan-out 0.0251, circular-flow 0.0164, rapid-churn 0.0150 — shows a pattern you
should internalize: **rare, structurally specific patterns are precise;
common, loosely structural ones are not.** The rules engine reports this
laundering-entity precision explicitly as a *tuning compass* (a signal for
where to tighten thresholds) rather than as a performance claim, because the
labels are only complete for known laundering accounts, not verified absent
from the rest.

## 5. From transaction data to accounts, and back

An operational monitor must decide two distinct questions, and the workbench
demonstrates both being wrong in instructive ways before being handled
carefully:

1. **Which transactions look illicit?** (Track A, per-transaction labels,
   supervised learning on features.)
2. **Which accounts are laundering?** (Track B, account-level labels,
   rule scenarios for behavior, a LightGBM model over account histories,
   then triage fuses both into one queue.)

The two questions do not collapse into each other because a bank does not
file a report on a transaction; it files one on (or about) a person or
account. And describing the account level cleanly is itself a modeling
problem — what window of prior history represents "the behavior before this
alert"? The triage stage answers it with as-of dates: each alert's ML features
come strictly from transaction history before the alert's window-end, which is
point-in-time discipline (see primer-ml.md, Section on leakage).

The triage report also shows the small detail that matters operationally:
alerts are deduplicated per (entity, day) keeping only the highest-scoring
reason, so that KPI cost arithmetic never double-counts investigations. When
scores are fused as $\text{fused} = 0.60 \times \text{ML} + 0.40 \times
\text{rule}$, an alert whose account has no prior history gets no ML component
and carries the rule score alone; it is recorded as such rather than imputed.
The number one alert in the queue (entity 8079DDC30, day 2022-09-11,
$\text{rule} = 0.0922$, $\text{ML} = 0.9762$) fuses to
$0.6 \times 0.9762 + 0.4 \times 0.0922 = 0.6226$, exactly as recorded.

## 6. Learning the domain: reading list

The reading list follows a single path from orientation to depth.

- **FATF, "International Standards on Combating Money Laundering" (the 40
  Recommendations)** — the global baseline of AML obligations, the document
  an EU-level regime ultimately serves. Read to see how customer due
  diligence, record keeping, and reporting fit together; free at fatf-gafi.org.
- **Regulation (EU) 2024/1624 and Directive (EU) 2024/1640** — the primary
  EU texts on EUR-Lex. Read the articles on risk assessment and transaction
  monitoring, not the whole thing.
- **EBA Guidelines EBA/GL/2021/02** — what "evidence the system is effective"
  means in audit terms; explains why a ranked, costed queue matters to a
  supervisor.
- **FFFS 2017:11** (Finansinspektionen) — the Swedish transposition, listed in
  the report's regulatory one-pager.
- **SR 11-7, "Supervisory Guidance on Model Risk Management"** (Fed/OCC,
  2011) — the model-governance layer; short, and every paragraph is quotable
  in an interview about validating a monitor.
- **Weber et al., "Anti-Money Laundering in Bitcoin: Experimenting with Graph
  Convolutional Networks for Financial Forensics" (arXiv:1908.02573, 2019)** —
  the paper that released Elliptic and set the benchmark the report compares
  against; read alongside the Track A results.
- **IBM AMLSim documentation and paper** (IBM Research, and the HI-Small
  Kaggle card) — how the synthetic generator encodes typologies as simulation
  programs; read to see what the Track B data can and cannot be evidence of.
- **Elliptic+, Elliptic2** — later enlargements of the Elliptic dataset with
  additional time steps and temporal label changes; the natural next study
  after this project.

The reading order that pays: FATF overview first (one evening), then the
scenario lists of HI-Small against the typologies in Section 4 (half an hour),
then the regulation only when you need to answer "who has to do this and
prove what".
